# Frame review, 18 November 2026

A second look at the Yearbook against the steerer's own Wrapped recording. The first comparison
(`YEARBOOK-VS-WRAPPED.md`) was made from the recording read by eye, in eighteen states. This one reads
**every frame** of both films, at a tenth of a second, and puts a number on each.

**The films.**

- The reference: `VID_20261102_013903_1.mp4`, 860 × 1920, 60 frames a second, **134.4 seconds**.
  A phone screen recording, so it carries a VEED watermark and the phone's status bar; neither is design.
- Ours: `artifacts/wrapped-recording/yearbook-portrait-2026-11-18.webm`, 390 × 844, 25 frames a second,
  recorded hands-off at phone size, the film advancing on its own clock. **295.5 seconds** to the closing
  card. The recording is longer than that only because nobody left the last card, so it sat for another
  four minutes; every measurement below stops where the film stops.

**How each frame was read.** Both films were cut into samples exactly 0.1 seconds apart — 1,344 for the
reference, 5,413 for ours. Each sample records brightness, saturation, red/green/blue, edge energy,
how much of the lower half is light or dark ink (the words), and how far it has moved from the sample
before it. Those tables are `reference/samples.csv` and `ours/samples.csv` beside this file. The sheets
in those folders show the frames themselves: twelve for the reference at every 0.1 second
(`sheet-01` is 0–11.9 s, `sheet-12` is 132–134.3 s) and ten for ours at every half second, because no
card of ours changes inside itself. Full-size stills are beside them.

**Not reviewed: the sound.** The reference has music and the whole film is cut to its beat; I cannot
hear either film, so the one thing it might be doing best is the one thing this review cannot score.

## What the review says is now right

The chrome is not an approximation; it is the same design. A dashed line across the top with the
current chapter marked, a rail down the left, pause and mute at the top right, a close, and a
"Share this story" pill at the foot. Ours matches all six, and the review of their frames is what
confirms it rather than my memory of it.

The closing screen is nearly theirs. A card with a title, two columns of lists and a number, a row of
colourways with dots under it, a Share button, and "Replay from beginning" — that is their screen, and
after Phase 4 it is ours. Ours has three colourways where they have six, and no photograph.

The chapter set is theirs: totals, places and distance, the ranking, the busiest day, the taste and
habit cards, the crew, and the card at the end.

## What is still different, in order of how much it shows

1. **The film barely moves.** Theirs changes a picture every 3.4 seconds and the camera never stops;
   ours sits on one arrangement for 15.6 seconds and drifts. This is the biggest gap and it is the one
   the first comparison called the "INSANE difference". Phase 1 made each card a real lit set; it did
   not give any of them something to *do*.
2. **There are no people.** About a third of their runtime is photography — album covers in a list, a
   band held for sixteen seconds. Ours has no photograph anywhere, and it is a yearbook.
3. **One sentence where they write two.** Every card of ours is one headline, set once. They put a
   small line above or below it, and they change the copy mid-scene — three sentences on one sunset.
   The one-sentence rule is the reason we do not have this, and it is the single rule that most makes
   the two films feel different.
4. **Ours is bright and flat; theirs is dark and lit.** Mean brightness 102 against 48. They work in
   black with pockets of colour; we work in full-bleed colour on every card.
5. **The pacing.** Nineteen cards in five minutes against fourteen parts in two and a quarter. A person
   watching ours has time to get bored; that is not a taste judgement, it is 15.6 seconds against 3.4.

## Two faults this review found in our own film

Both at full size in `ours/full-176s.png` and `reference/full-79s.png`.

1. **The share pill sits on top of the ranked list.** On our ranking card the pill covers the fourth
   row and the fifth row is off the bottom of the frame. Theirs fits all five above the pill, with a
   photograph on each row. Our check *requires* the fifth row to be cut by the frame, so the check is
   happy with exactly the thing that reads badly. Row 53 in `DEFECTS.md`.
2. **The ranking rows have letters where theirs have faces.** U, A, A, G in tiles, against five
   photographs. That one is not a bug; it is the cost of having no artwork, and it is why the ranking
   card looks like a table where theirs looks like a page.

## The score

**6 out of 10.**

Not because the structure is wrong — the structure is now close to theirs, and the controls are
theirs almost exactly. Six because a Wrapped film is a moving, photographed, written thing and ours is a
beautiful set of stills: it wins on the story and the chrome, and loses on motion, people, the second
line and the pace. Broken down: story and chapter set 8, controls and chrome 9, the closing card 8,
colour and light 6, movement 4, photography 2, copy and pacing 4.

To go from six to eight, in the order it would pay: give each card something that moves for its whole
life, put photography on the ranking and one held card, and let the copy change once inside a card.
The last of those needs the one-sentence rule lifted, and that is the steerer's decision.

## To repeat it

```
# 0.1-second samples, scene cuts and sheets for a whole film
python3 scripts/frame-review.py <film> design/wrapped-comparison/frame-review/<name> 120 0.1

# seconds wait: the fifth argument, 0, samples without writing sheets
python3 scripts/frame-review.py <film> <out> 120 0.1 0

# the side-by-side of the two sample tables
python3 scripts/frame-compare.py reference/samples.csv ours/samples.csv
```

Both are plain python with OpenCV, no other dependency, and neither touches the app. `frame-review.py`
reads a film frame by frame, measures every 0.1-second sample, finds the scene cuts and writes the
sheets; `frame-compare.py` cuts either sample table into cards and prints them side by side. The
recording was made with `scripts/record-wrapped.mjs`, with the arrow-key presses taken out so the film
ran on its own clock.
